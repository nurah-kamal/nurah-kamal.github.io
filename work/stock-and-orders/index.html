The question

A wholesaler sells 48 products from three warehouses. The questions are the ones a business actually asks: how did the year go, which products make the money, do suppliers deliver when they promise, what’s about to run out, and do new customers come back?

Each question is one SQL file, and the comment at the top of the file is the question. A test fails if a file doesn’t say what it asks.

What I found

One supplier, S04, started delivering late in March 2025. By the second half of the year, customers were going without about one in six of the units they ordered from its range.

The slip cost about R974,000 in lost sales in 2025, against R76,000 in 2024. It cuts across two categories, fasteners and plumbing, so it only shows once products are joined to their supplier. Every other supplier shipped more than 96.5% of what was ordered in every quarter of 2025.

The other questions turned up more:

  • Revenue was R115.6 million in 2024 and R169.9 million in 2025, at a 23% gross margin. 14 of the 48 products made 80% of 2025 revenue.
  • A Black Friday promotion brought in 235 new customers. 18.7% of them ordered again within 90 days; in every other month it was between 77% and 100%.
  • Paint sells the roller kit: about 45% of orders with wall, roof or primer paint include it, a lift of 3.5 to 3.9.
  • The top tenth of customers brought in 52.6% of 2025 revenue, and every one of them is a retail store.
  • Most of the slow stock by value is imported power tools, because the supplier’s minimum order is a month or more of sales at a single warehouse.

How I checked it

  • Two independent answers are made to agree. The generator tracks stock day by day in JavaScript; SQL rebuilds it from opening stock, deliveries and shipments. The tests compare the two for every product in all three warehouses, and check that a running ledger never goes below zero.
  • Planted, then found. The generator plants three things: a supplier that starts delivering late, a promotion that brings in customers who don’t come back, and a product pair bought together. Switch the plants off and those tests fail, while the rest still pass.
  • Not everything is planted. The slow stock comes from the supplier’s minimum order quantity, and the write-up says which findings were planted and which arose on their own.
  • Demand is what was ordered, not what shipped. Measured on shipped units, a stockout would hide itself.
  • The SQL is plain and readable: CTEs, joins and window functions (LAG, RANK, NTILE, running sums), with percentiles done by ranking because SQLite has none.

The principle behind it

Go back to the source, and test the answer as well as writing the query.

A query that runs isn’t the same as a query that’s right. Every answer here has a test, and the stock figures are checked against a second, independent calculation.

Worth knowing

  • The data is synthetic and says nothing about any real business. No person is in it: a customer is an ID, a segment, a region and a date.
  • Whatever a warehouse can’t ship on the day is cancelled, not back-ordered.
  • Money is in rand, excluding VAT, with prices fixed for the two years.
